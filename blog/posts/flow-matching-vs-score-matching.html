DIFFUSION MATH

Flow Matching vs. Score Matching: Same Model, Different Knobs

// what actually differs, and why the improvements work

01 // INTRODUCTION

Somewhere around 2024 the generative modeling world quietly changed its vocabulary. Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux are "rectified flow transformers". Most new video generation models train with "flow matching". Physical Intelligence's π0 generates robot actions with a "flow matching action expert". If you learned diffusion through score matching like I did, the natural question is: did something fundamental change, or did we just rename the parts?

The short answer, which the rest of this post earns: whenever generation starts from Gaussian noise, flow matching and score-based diffusion are the same model family, whatever the data looks like. Same marginals, same optimal network (up to a fixed linear map), same family of samplers. Every difference you can measure comes from four knobs that both frameworks expose:

  1. The path: the noise schedule $(\alpha_t, \sigma_t)$ that decides how data and noise are mixed over time.
  2. The prediction target: whether the network predicts the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, the clean data $\mathbf{x}_0$, the score $\nabla\log p_t$, the "$\mathbf{v}$-prediction" target $\alpha_t\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \sigma_t\mathbf{x}_0$ (Salimans & Ho), or the flow velocity $\mathbf{u}$ (section 04 has the full dictionary).
  3. The weighting: how much training cares about each noise level, which is set jointly by the loss weight at that level and by how often that level is sampled during training.
  4. The sampler: ODE or SDE, which solver, and how many steps.

Flow matching's practical wins are real, but they mostly come from shipping better defaults for these knobs, plus a cleaner mental model that made new ideas (couplings, reflow, learning the flow map) easier to think of. The two frameworks genuinely part ways only when you leave the Gaussian case: arbitrary source distributions, data-to-data transport, manifolds, discrete tokens.

This is a technical post. I'll assume you know denoising score matching and the probability flow ODE at the level of the previous post. Everything else is derived here.

"Flow matching is diffusion with the right defaults and a better story. The better story turned out to matter."

02 // THE SCORE SIDE IN ONE SCREEN

One notation for the whole post. A Gaussian probability path mixes a data point $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim q$ with noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad t \in [0, 1]$$

with $\alpha_0 = 1, \sigma_0 = 0$ (clean data) and $\alpha_1 \approx 0, \sigma_1 \approx 1$ (pure noise). Time runs from data ($t=0$) to noise ($t=1$), as in the score SDE paper and SD3. (Lipman et al. use the opposite direction; I've flipped their formulas to match.) The single most useful scalar is the log signal-to-noise ratio:

$$\lambda_t = \log \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}$$

It decreases monotonically from $+\infty$ (data) to $-\infty$ (noise). Two schedules we'll keep coming back to:

  • Variance preserving (VP), cosine: $\alpha_t = \cos(\tfrac{\pi t}{2})$, $\sigma_t = \sin(\tfrac{\pi t}{2})$. The DDPM / improved-DDPM family.
  • Rectified flow (RF) / linear: $\alpha_t = 1 - t$, $\sigma_t = t$. The flow matching default.

Why the schedule matters if it's "just coordinates"

Section 04 will show that, for a perfectly trained model, the schedule is only a change of coordinates: same $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ at the same $\lambda$. So why do papers fight over it? Because in practice the schedule quietly decides three things.

  1. The shape of the path in $\mathbf{x}$-space. VP moves $\mathbf{x}_t$ along an arc that keeps its overall scale ($\alpha_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1$). RF moves along the straight chord, so mid-path samples shrink: at $t = 0.5$ the scale is $1/\sqrt{2}$ of the endpoints. So the network sees inputs of a different overall size at different times.
  2. Where training spends its samples. Drawing $t$ uniformly is the default in both camps, so the schedule decides which noise levels the network practices most (section 05).
  3. Where a sampler spends its steps. A sampler on a uniform $t$ grid lands on whatever noise levels the schedule maps those times to (section 06).

The figures below show all three. The surprise is in the second one: as functions of time, RF and VP are almost the same schedule in log-SNR. Their big practical differences come from the geometry and from what was bundled with them (the prediction target and the timestep density), not from where they put the noise levels.

From the score side, we train $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}, t) \approx \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$ with denoising score matching, then sample with the reverse SDE or the probability flow ODE. For the path above, the forward SDE has drift $f_t \mathbf{x}$ and diffusion $g_t$ with

$$f_t = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}, \qquad g_t^2 = \frac{d\sigma_t^2}{dt} - 2 f_t \sigma_t^2 = -\sigma_t^2 \dot\lambda_t$$

and the probability flow ODE is

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = f_t \mathbf{x} - \tfrac{1}{2} g_t^2 \, \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x} + \frac{\sigma_t^2 \dot\lambda_t}{2} \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$$

Hold on to that last expression. It is going to reappear from a completely different direction.

03 // FLOW MATCHING FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES

Continuous normalizing flows, without the simulation

A continuous normalizing flow (CNF) moves samples with an ODE $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x})$. If $\mathbf{x}$ starts with density $p_1$, its density $p_t$ evolves by the continuity equation:

$$\partial_t p_t(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla \cdot \big(p_t(\mathbf{x})\, \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x})\big) = 0$$

CNFs were trained by maximum likelihood, which means integrating the ODE (and a divergence trace) inside every training step. Expensive and unstable. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) flips the problem around: pick a probability path $p_t$ that connects noise to data, find a vector field $\mathbf{u}_t$ that generates it, and simply regress onto it:

$$\mathcal{L}_{FM}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, \mathbf{x} \sim p_t} \left\| \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}, t) - \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}) \right\|^2$$

No ODE solves during training. The catch is the same one score matching hit in 2005: the target $\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x})$ depends on the unknown data distribution.

The conditioning trick (again)

Build the marginal path out of simple per-sample paths. For each data point $\mathbf{x}_0$, define a conditional path $p_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_0)$ with a known conditional field $\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{x}_0)$. For our Gaussian path the conditional trajectory is $\mathbf{x}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ and its velocity is simply its time derivative:

$$\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0) = \dot\alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \dot\sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{RF}} \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \mathbf{x}_0$$

Lipman et al. prove two facts. First, the marginal field, defined as the posterior average of conditional fields,

$$\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}\left[\, \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0) \;\middle|\; \mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x} \right] = \dot\alpha_t\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0 | \mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}] + \dot\sigma_t\, \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon} | \mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}]$$

generates the marginal path $p_t$ (plug it into the continuity equation and the per-sample equations average out). Second, the conditional flow matching loss

$$\mathcal{L}_{CFM}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, \mathbf{x}_0,\, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left\| \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - (\dot\alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \dot\sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \right\|^2$$

differs from $\mathcal{L}_{FM}$ by a constant that doesn't depend on $\theta$. The proof is three lines: expand both squares; the $\|\mathbf{v}_\theta\|^2$ terms match; the cross terms match by the tower property, because $\mathbb{E}[\langle \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t), \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0)\rangle] = \mathbb{E}[\langle \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t), \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0)|\mathbf{x}_t]\rangle]$; what's left doesn't involve $\theta$.

DÉJÀ VU

This is exactly Vincent's 2011 argument for denoising score matching. DSM regresses onto the conditional score $\nabla \log p(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0)$, whose posterior mean is the marginal score. CFM regresses onto the conditional velocity, whose posterior mean is the marginal velocity. Same trick, different regression target. Both methods are least-squares estimators of a posterior mean, and that is the root of everything that follows.

Rectified flow and stochastic interpolants

Two concurrent papers reached the same place from other directions. Rectified flow (Liu, Gong & Liu, 2023) starts from any coupling of two distributions, draws straight lines between paired points, and fits the velocity by least squares. Its distinctive contribution is reflow: re-pair the points using the trained ODE and train again. More on that in section 06. Stochastic interpolants (Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023; Albergo, Boffi & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023) generalize further:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = I(t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1) + \gamma_t \mathbf{z}, \qquad (\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1) \sim \pi(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1), \quad \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

Any two endpoint distributions, any coupling $\pi$, any interpolation function, and an optional latent noise $\gamma_t \mathbf{z}$. The framework shows that the same marginals can be produced by an ODE or by a family of SDEs with tunable noise, and that both drifts are minimizers of simple quadratic losses. Gaussian-source flow matching is the special case $\mathbf{x}_1 = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, independent coupling, $\gamma_t = 0$.

04 // THE DICTIONARY: ONE POSTERIOR MEAN, FIVE OUTFITS

Since $\mathbf{x}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ holds exactly, the two posterior means are tied together: $\mathbf{x}_t = \alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sigma_t \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0 | \mathbf{x}_t]$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon} | \mathbf{x}_t]$. Given $\mathbf{x}_t$, one vector determines the other. And Tweedie's formula ties the score to the same object:

$$\nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t) = -\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}{\sigma_t} = -\frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0}{\sigma_t^2}$$

So every popular network output is a fixed, time-dependent linear function of $(\mathbf{x}_t, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)$:

Target Definition Optimal value Used by
$\mathbf{x}_0$ clean data $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ EDM (via preconditioning), JiT
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ noise $(\mathbf{x}_t - \alpha_t\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)/\sigma_t$ DDPM, SD 1.x
score $\nabla \log p_t$ $-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}/\sigma_t$ NCSN, score SDE
$\mathbf{v}$ $\alpha_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \sigma_t \mathbf{x}_0$ (VP) $\alpha_t \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \sigma_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ progressive distillation, SD 2.x
$\mathbf{u}$ $\dot\alpha_t \mathbf{x}_0 + \dot\sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ $\dot\alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \dot\sigma_t \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ flow matching, RF, SD3, π0

$\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}$ are close cousins: on the VP cosine path, $\mathbf{u} = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{v}$ exactly, which is why $\mathbf{v}$-prediction is often described as "predicting the velocity". A perfectly trained network of one kind is a perfectly trained network of every other kind. Now substitute $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = (\mathbf{x}_t + \sigma_t^2 \nabla \log p_t)/\alpha_t$ into the velocity:

$$\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x} + \left(\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\sigma_t^2 - \sigma_t\dot\sigma_t\right) \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x} + \frac{\sigma_t^2 \dot\lambda_t}{2} \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$$
THE PUNCHLINE

This is the probability flow ODE from section 02, term for term. The flow matching velocity field is the probability flow drift. Training a Gaussian-path flow matching model trains a score model in a different parameterization; integrating its ODE is running the probability flow ODE. For rectified flow it simplifies to $$\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{\mathbf{x} + t\, \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})}{1-t}$$

One more consequence that matters in practice: the schedule itself is mostly a change of coordinates. Two Gaussian paths with the same $\lambda$ range differ only by a rescaling $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{x}/(\alpha_t + \sigma_t)$ and a reparameterization of time. For RF, $\lambda_t = 2\log\frac{1-t}{t}$, i.e. $t = \text{sigmoid}(-\lambda/2)$. The linear path is one more point in the space of noise schedules diffusion people already explored, not a new kind of object (Kingma & Gao, 2023; Gao et al., 2024).

05 // LOSSES ARE WEIGHTED ELBOs

If the optimal networks coincide, the losses must differ only in how they weight errors at each noise level. Let's make that precise. Write every loss in terms of the error of an implied $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$. Since all targets are linear in $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ given $\mathbf{x}_t$, an error $\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ becomes a scaled error in every other target. Using $\sigma_t / \alpha_t = e^{-\lambda/2}$:

Loss on Error relation Weight on $\|\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^2$
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-MSE $\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ $1$
$\mathbf{x}_0$-MSE $\delta\mathbf{x}_0 = -\tfrac{\sigma_t}{\alpha_t}\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ $e^{-\lambda}$
$\mathbf{v}$-MSE (VP) $\delta\mathbf{v} = \delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}/\alpha_t$ $1 + e^{-\lambda}$
$\mathbf{u}$-MSE (RF) $\delta\mathbf{u} = (1 + \tfrac{\sigma_t}{\alpha_t})\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ $(1 + e^{-\lambda/2})^2$
DSM with $\lambda(\sigma) = \sigma^2$ $\delta\mathbf{s} = -\delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}/\sigma_t$ $1$

That's half the story. The other half is which noise levels you sample. Training draws $t$ from some density, which induces a density $p(\lambda)$ over log-SNR. The full objective is

$$\mathcal{L} = \int p(\lambda)\, w(\lambda)\; \mathbb{E}\left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\lambda, \lambda) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right\|^2 d\lambda$$

and only the product $p(\lambda)\,w(\lambda)$ matters for the expected gradient.

SAME TARGET, DIFFERENT NOISE

If only the product matters, is raising the weight the same as sampling more often? In expectation, yes. In a finite minibatch, no: the split is a choice of Monte Carlo estimator. Moving emphasis into $w$ means rare samples with large weights, so the gradient is spiky; moving it into $p$ means more samples at that level, each with an ordinary weight. This is importance sampling: for a fixed product, the variance is lowest when $p(\lambda)$ follows the product itself (scaled by how noisy the per-level gradients are), leaving $w/p$ close to flat. Adam makes this sharper still: its per-parameter normalization is calibrated by the typical gradient, so occasional heavily weighted samples either get washed out or destabilize the second-moment estimate. That's why modern recipes like SD3's prefer to change the sampling density and keep the per-sample loss simple.

Let's compute the product for vanilla rectified flow, $t \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1]$. From $t = \text{sigmoid}(-\lambda/2)$:

$$p(\lambda) = \left|\frac{dt}{d\lambda}\right| = \frac{1}{8}\,\text{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{4}\right), \qquad w(\lambda) = (1 + e^{-\lambda/2})^2 = 4 e^{-\lambda/2}\cosh^2\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{4}\right)$$
$$\Longrightarrow \quad p(\lambda)\, w(\lambda) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-\lambda/2}$$

The cosh terms cancel exactly. Vanilla rectified flow is $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-prediction with an effective weight $e^{-\lambda/2}$, which decreases monotonically with SNR. That brings us to Kingma & Gao (2023), the most underrated paper in this whole story.

KINGMA & GAO, IN ONE SENTENCE

Every weighted diffusion loss is an integral of ELBOs over noise levels, and when the effective weight is monotonically decreasing in $\lambda$, the loss is exactly the ELBO of the data under Gaussian-noise data augmentation. Rectified flow's $e^{-\lambda/2}$ is monotone, so vanilla flow matching is a likelihood objective in disguise. Their Table 1 lists the same $e^{-\lambda/2}$ for $\mathbf{v}$-prediction with the cosine schedule (another quiet equivalence), while $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-prediction with the cosine schedule ($\text{sech}(\lambda/2)$) and EDM ($\mathcal{N}(\lambda; 2.4, 2.4^2)(e^{-\lambda} + 0.52)$) are not monotone. They then reached state-of-the-art ImageNet FIDs while searching only over monotone weightings.

Now look at what SD3 actually ships. Esser et al. (2024) ran a large sweep of formulations and timestep samplers for text-to-image and the winner was rectified flow with logit-normal timestep sampling: $\text{logit}(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$. Since $\text{logit}(t) = -\lambda/2$, that is simply

$$\lambda \sim \mathcal{N}(-2m,\; 4s^2)$$

With their default $m=0, s=1$, $\lambda$ has standard deviation 2. Uniform $t$ gives a logistic density in $\lambda$ with standard deviation $2\pi/\sqrt{3} \approx 3.6$. So the famous trick is: spend less compute on the nearly-clean and nearly-pure-noise ends, and concentrate it around $\lambda \approx 0$, where the network decides what the image actually contains. The effective weight is no longer monotone, so this is a deliberate move away from likelihood toward perceptual quality.

The same lens explains a whole shelf of papers. Min-SNR weighting (Hang et al., 2023) clips $w$ at high SNR. EDM samples $\log\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$, a Gaussian in $\lambda$ again. Lee et al. (2024) use a U-shaped timestep density for reflow, putting mass at both ends because a near-straight flow's errors live there. π0 uses a Beta distribution that favors the noisy end for actions. Different papers, one function being tuned: $p(\lambda)w(\lambda)$.

06 // GEOMETRY: THE MYTH OF STRAIGHT PATHS

The most repeated claim about flow matching is that its paths are straight, so it samples in fewer steps. The conditional paths are straight: each $\mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\mathbf{x}_0 + t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is a line segment. The marginal ODE, the thing you actually integrate, is not, and there's a clean way to see exactly when it bends.

Curvature is the posterior changing its mind

Along an RF trajectory, $\mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + t\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_t = \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$, where the hats are evaluated along the path. Differentiate the first identity and subtract the second: $(1-t)\dot{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_0 + t\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}} = 0$. Then

$$\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_t = \dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}} - \dot{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_0 = -\frac{1}{t}\,\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0}{dt}$$

The path accelerates exactly when the denoiser's estimate of the clean sample changes. At high noise, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ is a blurry average of everything plausible. As noise drops, the posterior commits to one mode, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ swings toward it, and the trajectory turns. With multimodal data and an independent coupling, that turn is unavoidable: ODE trajectories cannot cross, while the straight conditional lines they average over cross all the time.

The same view explains why Gao et al. (2024) found that VP paths can be straighter than RF paths on some data: straightness is a property of how $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ evolves along the path, which depends on the data distribution and the schedule together, not on the conditional paths being lines.

Euler on rectified flow is DDIM

The DDIM update freezes the current predictions and jumps along the path: $\mathbf{x}_s = \alpha_s \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sigma_s \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$. Under RF, one Euler step from $t$ to $s$ gives

$$\mathbf{x}_s = \mathbf{x}_t + (s - t)(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0) = (1-s)\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + s\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$$

which is the DDIM update with the RF schedule. And because DDIM depends on the schedule only through the noise-to-signal ratio at each step, DDIM on any Gaussian schedule is Euler on the RF ODE after rescaling $\mathbf{x}$ by $1/(\alpha_t + \sigma_t)$ and mapping time to $\sigma_t/(\alpha_t + \sigma_t)$. A lot of "flow matching samples faster" is "people stopped using the plain Euler–Maruyama DDPM sampler and started using what amounts to DDIM". DDIM is exact whenever $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ stays constant, which by the identity above is precisely when the path is straight.

What actually straightens paths: the coupling

If curvature comes from posterior uncertainty about which $\mathbf{x}_0$ a noise sample belongs to, the fix is to reduce that uncertainty, by choosing which noise goes with which data point during training.

  • Minibatch optimal transport (Tong et al., 2024; Pooladian et al., 2023): pair noise and data within each batch by solving a small OT problem. Conditional lines cross less, so the marginal field bends less. Effective in low dimensions; the benefit fades as batch size becomes tiny relative to the data's complexity.
  • Reflow (Liu et al., 2023): integrate the trained ODE to get deterministic pairs $(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \text{ODE}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))$, then train a new flow on those pairs. Because the pairs come from a flow, their lines don't cross in the limit, and the new flow is straighter with provably non-increasing transport cost. Lee et al. (2024) showed one round is nearly enough, if you fix the timestep density and the loss (U-shaped $t$, LPIPS-Huber).
  • Distill the flow map instead of straightening the field. That is the consistency / MeanFlow family in section 09.

07 // STOCHASTICITY: ODE vs SDE

Flow matching models are usually sampled deterministically, but nothing forces that. For any $\beta_t \ge 0$, the reverse-time SDE

$$d\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}) - \frac{\beta_t^2}{2}\nabla_\mathbf{x}\log p_t(\mathbf{x})\right]dt + \beta_t\, d\bar{\mathbf{w}}$$

(integrated from $t=1$ down to $0$) has the same marginals $p_t$: the extra drift and the injected noise cancel in the Fokker–Planck equation. $\beta_t = 0$ is the flow ODE; $\beta_t = g_t$ is Anderson's reverse SDE; anything in between is EDM's "churn". With a Gaussian source, the score comes free from the velocity via the identity in section 04, so every flow matching model is also an SDE sampler, and every diffusion model is also a flow.

Why would you want noise? Because the network is never perfect. A deterministic ODE carries every early error to the end, while the Langevin-like part of the SDE pulls the sample distribution back toward $p_t$ at each step. Albergo, Boffi & Vanden-Eijnden make this precise: with stochastic dynamics, the KL divergence of the generated distribution is controlled by the quadratic training losses; for the deterministic ODE that control is much weaker. The price is that stochastic samplers need more steps; at very low step counts the ODE wins.

One modern reason to care: RL fine-tuning. Policy-gradient methods need a stochastic policy with tractable step likelihoods, and a deterministic ODE has neither. Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) converts the flow ODE into an equivalent-marginal SDE exactly as above, which turns each denoising step into a Gaussian policy step that GRPO can optimize.

08 // IN PRACTICE: WHAT MATTERS AT SCALE

Timestep density and the resolution shift

At higher resolution, the same per-pixel noise destroys less information: average a $2\times 2$ block of pixels and the noise standard deviation halves while the signal stays, so the coarse structure has 4× the SNR. Hoogeboom et al. (2023) argued the schedule should shift the SNR by the ratio of resolutions squared; SD3 shifts timesteps by

$$t' = \frac{a\, t}{1 + (a - 1)\, t}, \qquad a = \sqrt{m/n}$$

where $m/n$ is the ratio of pixel counts (they use $a = 3$ at $1024^2$). In log-SNR this is a pure translation: $\text{logit}(t') = \text{logit}(t) + \log a$, i.e.

$$\lambda' = \lambda - \log\frac{m}{n}$$

which is exactly Hoogeboom's rule. The two recipes are the same idea, written in different coordinates. Video models apply the same shift for the extra frames. The lesson is general: specify noise in $\lambda$, and shift it when the data's redundancy changes.

Prediction targets and numerical conditioning

All targets have the same optimum but very different error amplification, and that is where real differences appear:

  • $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-prediction breaks at the noisy end. Recovering $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = (\mathbf{x}_t - \sigma_t\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})/\alpha_t$ divides by $\alpha_t \to 0$. At exactly zero terminal SNR it's undefined, which is why common DDPM schedules left residual signal at $t=1$, and why models trained that way can't make very dark or very bright images (Lin et al., 2024). Their fix: zero terminal SNR plus $\mathbf{v}$-prediction.
  • $\mathbf{x}_0$-prediction breaks at the clean end, symmetrically: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ divides by $\sigma_t \to 0$.
  • $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}$ are bounded at both ends. $\mathbf{u} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \mathbf{x}_0$ has the same scale everywhere. That alone makes flow matching a robust default, which is a big part of why it "just works". EDM's preconditioning ($c_\text{skip}, c_\text{out}$) achieves the same conditioning in a diffusion parameterization.
  • In very high dimensions, predict the data. Li & He (2025) train plain ViTs on raw pixels with large patches (JiT). Under the manifold hypothesis, clean images live on a low-dimensional manifold, while $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are full-dimensional; a network whose width is smaller than the patch dimension simply cannot output them. $\mathbf{x}_0$-prediction works there where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$- and $\mathbf{v}$-prediction fail badly. Prediction target and loss space are separate choices: you can predict $\mathbf{x}_0$ and still compute the loss on the implied velocity.

Guidance carries over exactly

The velocity is affine in the score, and the affine part $\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}$ does not depend on the condition $\mathbf{c}$. So

$$\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x}|\varnothing) = \frac{\sigma_t^2\dot\lambda_t}{2}\Big(\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{c}) - \nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{x})\Big)$$

and classifier-free guidance on velocities, $\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{u}_\varnothing + w(\mathbf{u}_\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{u}_\varnothing)$, is identical to CFG on scores. All the folklore about guidance scales, oversaturation and guidance intervals transfers unchanged.

Robotics: why action generation moved to flow matching

Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) generated robot action sequences with a DDPM-style $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-prediction model and showed that diffusion handles the multimodality of demonstrations far better than regression. π0 (Black et al., 2024) uses flow matching instead: a separate "action expert" attached to a VLM backbone predicts the velocity for a 50-step action chunk, integrated with 10 Euler steps, at up to 50 Hz control. Its timestep density is a Beta distribution that emphasizes the noisy end, the opposite of image models' bias toward the middle.

Through the lens of this post, every one of those choices is one of the four knobs. The bounded $\mathbf{u}$ target makes training robust. Euler on RF is DDIM, so 10 steps behave like a well-tuned 10-step DDIM. And the Beta density is a statement about where the hard decisions are: once the robot's observations are in context, the fine detail of an action chunk is nearly determined; what's hard is choosing the mode (go left or right around the cup), and that choice happens at low SNR. (That last interpretation is mine, not the paper's.)

09 // A UNIFYING LENS ON IMPROVEMENTS

With the equivalence in hand, most improvements from either camp sort into four knobs, plus one genuinely new direction.

Knob Methods Why it works
Path / coupling OT-CFM, multisample FM, reflow, Schrödinger bridges Reduce posterior uncertainty about $\mathbf{x}_0$, so $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ moves less and paths bend less.
Prediction target $\mathbf{v}$-pred, EDM preconditioning, RF's $\mathbf{u}$, JiT's $\mathbf{x}_0$ Keep the network's output well-scaled at every noise level and inside what the architecture can represent.
Weighting × density logit-normal, Min-SNR, EDM log-normal, U-shaped, resolution shift Put capacity on the noise levels that matter for the metric you care about. Monotone weights are ELBOs; others trade likelihood for perception.
Sampler DDIM, DPM-Solver, Heun, churn Higher-order or exponential integrators exploit the semi-linear structure; noise injection corrects accumulated error.
Learn the flow map consistency models, sCM, shortcut models, MeanFlow Learn the integral of the field, not the field. Few-step quality no longer depends on straightness.

The new direction: learn the integral, not the derivative

Everything so far learns the instantaneous velocity $\mathbf{u}_t(\mathbf{x})$ and leaves integration to a solver. The flow-map family learns the solver's output directly: a function $\mathbf{X}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, s)$ that maps a point at time $t$ to where the ODE would put it at time $s$.

  • Consistency models (Song et al., 2023) learn the map straight to $s = 0$ by enforcing that all points on one trajectory map to the same output. sCM (Lu & Song, 2025) makes the continuous-time version stable, partly through a "TrigFlow" parameterization that unifies EDM and flow matching, and scales it to 1.5B parameters with 2-step sampling.
  • Shortcut models (Frans et al., 2025) condition the network on the step size $d$ and enforce self-consistency: two steps of size $d$ equal one step of size $2d$.
  • MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025) learns the average velocity $\bar{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_t, s, t) = \frac{1}{t-s}\int_s^t \mathbf{u}_\tau\, d\tau$. Differentiating the definition gives an identity, $\bar{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{u} - (t-s)\frac{d}{dt}\bar{\mathbf{u}}$, that the network is trained to satisfy using a Jacobian-vector product, from scratch, with no distillation. It reaches FID 3.43 on ImageNet 256 with a single network evaluation.

Notice how naturally these are phrased in flow language: velocities, average velocities, step sizes. That's the real legacy of flow matching. It didn't change what the model learns in the Gaussian case, but it gave us coordinates in which the next idea is easier to see.

One family sits outside the knobs entirely: representation alignment (REPA, Yu et al., 2025) adds a loss that aligns intermediate features with a pretrained encoder like DINOv2, and speeds up training of both diffusion and flow transformers by a large factor. It changes what the network learns internally, not the generative framework, which is another reminder that the framework is rarely the bottleneck.

10 // WHERE THEY GENUINELY DIFFER

Everything above assumed a Gaussian source and an independent coupling. Remove those and the two frameworks stop being the same:

  • Data-to-data transport. Image-to-image translation, super-resolution from a low-res source, mapping one cell population to another: the source is not Gaussian, so there is no "score of the noised data" to speak of. Flow matching and stochastic interpolants handle any pair of distributions and any coupling directly; diffusion needs bridge constructions to do the same.
  • Manifolds. Riemannian flow matching (Chen & Lipman, 2024) needs only geodesics or a premetric on the manifold, and stays simulation-free on simple geometries. Riemannian diffusion requires heat kernels or simulation.
  • Discrete data. Discrete flow matching (Gat et al., 2024) builds probability paths over tokens with continuous-time Markov chains, alongside masked diffusion models. Here the "same model, different knobs" equivalence doesn't straightforwardly apply, and the design space is still being mapped.
  • When you need the score itself. Langevin correction steps, energy-based composition, guidance by an external likelihood, exact SDE likelihoods: these need $\nabla\log p_t$. With a Gaussian source you can always recover it from $\mathbf{u}$; with an arbitrary source you can't.

11 // TAKEAWAYS

  1. Same model, different knobs. With a Gaussian source, the flow matching velocity is the probability flow drift: $\mathbf{u}_t = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x} + \frac{\sigma_t^2\dot\lambda_t}{2}\nabla\log p_t$.
  2. Losses are weighted ELBOs. Compare objectives by $p(\lambda)w(\lambda)$. Vanilla RF is $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-MSE with weight $e^{-\lambda/2}$, a monotone, likelihood-flavored weighting; logit-normal is a deliberate move toward mid-SNR.
  3. Straightness is about the coupling, not the line. Paths bend when $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ changes. Euler on RF is DDIM. Real straightening needs OT couplings, reflow, or learning the flow map.
  4. Defaults matter more than frameworks. If you're starting fresh: $\mathbf{u}$ or $\mathbf{v}$ target (or $\mathbf{x}_0$ in very high-dimensional pixel space), logit-normal timesteps with a resolution shift, Euler or a second-order ODE solver, CFG on velocities, and a stochastic sampler if you have the step budget or need RL.
  5. Reach for flow language when you leave the Gaussian world: data-to-data, manifolds, discrete tokens, flow maps. That's where it's more than a renaming.

In the previous post, two communities converged on the score from opposite directions. This time the convergence happened again, one level up: flow matching people rediscovered diffusion through ODEs and optimal transport, diffusion people recognized their own probability flow ODE, and both are now better for it.
